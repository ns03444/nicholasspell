From Network Scripts to Production Software

I still know Ansible. I still know how to sit on a device and make it do the right thing. That is not what I want a visitor to this site to think I do all day.

The useful career story is the move from “network scripting” to “building automation products.” SWIMv1 to SWIMv2 is the cleanest example I have of that move.

What the first version taught me

SWIMv1 lived close to Client WAN and Cisco workflows. Playbooks, templates, scripts — the standard toolkit. It automated real work. It also encoded too many assumptions about one domain into the shape of the tool. When the requirement became multi-vendor scale, the old shape fought back.

That phase was not wasted. You cannot design a platform for operations you have never done by hand or by playbook. You can get stuck there, though, and keep adding YAML until the system is unownable.

What the second version required

SWIMv2 needed the boring virtues of application software: a UI people can use, backend services, APIs, background jobs, parallel execution, validation, observability, and integration with the systems the enterprise already uses to approve work. It needed a roadmap and PBIs, not a folder of one-off jobs. It needed someone to sit between stakeholders, contractors, and the codebase and keep the architecture from dissolving into special cases.

That is a different job description than “writes automation.” It is closer to software-oriented infrastructure engineering. You still need the network depth. You also need to think in interfaces, state, and products.

How to tell the difference

If the artifact is a script, the user is you. If the artifact is a platform, the users are operators who were not in the design review. They need a workflow, a ticket, a log, and a way to run 1,000 devices without inventing a process that night.

I do not claim I built every line. I do claim I helped lead the direction and I write the software. Both halves are the point. The rest of this site — Connect, libraries, even the older Django and Flask work — is evidence of the same trajectory, with SWIM as the proof that it reached production scale.
